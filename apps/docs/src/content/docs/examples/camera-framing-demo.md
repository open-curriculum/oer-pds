**Subject:** Camera framing in 3D animation — shot types, focal length, and spatial storytelling.

**Learning objective (shared across all four):** Learners will be able to identify and intentionally apply six camera shot types in service of a narrative goal.

### Student-facing brief

**Camera Framing: Shot Types — Lesson 3 of 12**

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to identify the six standard camera shot types and apply them correctly in a produced exercise.

**Part 1 — Watch the demonstration.**
Your instructor will demonstrate each shot type using a prepared scene in Blender. For each shot: name the type, note what it shows, and note when you would use it.

**Part 2 — Complete the guided exercise.**
Open the provided scene file. Using the shot type reference sheet, set up each of the six shot types in sequence. Label each camera object with the shot type name. Submit the `.blend` file.

**Part 3 — Independent exercise.**
Open the new scene file (unfamiliar characters and environment). Set up all six shot types again without the reference sheet. Submit for grading.

**Assessment:** Automatic-check rubric — each of the six shot types is correct or not correct. Score of 5/6 or higher passes.

### Student-facing brief

**Camera Language Production Brief**

In this project, you will study camera language in a film of your choice, produce a 30-second scene that demonstrates intentional shot selection, bring it to group critique, and revise.

**Phase 1 — Observe and analyze.**
Select a 3–5 minute sequence from any film or animation. Identify and document every camera shot type used. Note: what does each shot choice reveal? What would change if the shot were different?

**Phase 2 — Production brief.**
Produce a 30-second scene (any subject) that demonstrates your intentional use of at least four different shot types. Write a one-paragraph intent statement before you begin. Your choices should serve a narrative or emotional goal.

**Phase 3 — Critique.**
Bring your work-in-progress to critique week. You will give and receive feedback structured around: Does the shot selection serve the stated intent? What is working? What is unclear?

**Phase 4 — Revision and reflection.**
Revise your scene based on at least two pieces of feedback. Document which feedback you used, which you didn't, and why. Submit: final scene + revision memo + reflection on your design decisions.

**Assessment:** Artifact quality (40%) + intent alignment (30%) + revision reasoning (30%).

### Student-facing brief

**Camera Framing, Power, and the Gaze — Analysis and Response**

Camera framing is not neutral. The choice of shot type, angle, and focus constructs what the audience sees, who they identify with, and who holds power in the scene. In this assignment, you will analyze and respond.

**Part 1 — Observation (before context).**
Watch the provided 4-minute sequence from *WALL-E* (2008). Without any background reading, note: whose perspective are you placed in? Who has visual dominance? What do you notice about how the camera relates to each character?

**Part 2 — Contextual analysis.**
Read the excerpts on spectatorship theory and visual power. Re-watch the sequence. How does the camera construct WALL-E's interiority? How does EVE's introduction shift spectatorial positioning? Cite specific shots.

**Part 3 — Close reading.**
Select any 60-second sequence from a film, animation, game, or interactive media. Conduct a formal shot analysis (label every shot type, angle, and length). Write a 500-word critical analysis: What does this framing construct? Who is positioned as subject? Who is positioned as object?

**Part 4 — Creative response.**
Produce a 30-second scene (any medium) that deliberately subverts the framing conventions you identified. Write a 200-word artist statement explaining your choices and their critical intent.

**Assessment:** Critical analysis rigor (40%) + evidence-anchored claims (30%) + creative response intentionality (30%).

### Student-facing brief

**Camera Framing Skills Track — Checkpoints 3.1–3.3**

You must demonstrate mastery at each checkpoint before advancing. Mastery = 90% or higher, demonstrated without reference materials.

---

**Checkpoint 3.1 — Identification**

*Mastery criteria:* Correctly identify all six standard shot types when viewing a provided reel.

*Practice:* Complete the labeled identification exercises in Module 3.1 (unlimited attempts).

*Checkpoint task:* You will be shown 18 unlabeled shots. Identify the shot type for each. 16/18 or higher = mastery. Below 16 = not yet. Review feedback, additional practice available, re-attempt when ready.

---

**Checkpoint 3.2 — Technical Setup**

*Mastery criteria:* Correctly set up all six shot types in a provided scene file.

*Practice:* Complete the guided setup exercises in Module 3.2. Reference sheet available during practice only.

*Checkpoint task:* Set up all six shot types in a new scene file. No reference sheet. All six correct = mastery. Any incorrect = not yet.

---

**Checkpoint 3.3 — Intentional Application**

*Mastery criteria:* Produce a 30-second scene that demonstrates intentional use of at least four shot types, with a written rationale for each choice.

*Practice:* Complete the optional application exercises in Module 3.3.

*Checkpoint task:* Produce a 30-second scene and submit with a shot rationale document. Evaluated by instructor against rubric. All criteria at proficient or above = mastery.
